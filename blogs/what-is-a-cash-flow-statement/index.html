    What Is a Cash Flow Statement? How It Works, Examples & Analysis

    A cash flow statement is a financial report that shows how cash moves into and out of a business over a specific period, such as a month, quarter, or year. It tracks money earned and spent across three areas: operating, investing, and financing activities, and it explains why a company's cash balance changed from the start of that period to the end.

    Understanding cash movement matters just as much as understanding profit. A business can look profitable on paper and still struggle to pay rent, payroll, or suppliers if cash isn't coming in fast enough. For Kansas business owners managing seasonal revenue, equipment costs, or growing teams, a clear view of operating, investing, and financing cash flow can make the difference between confidently planning ahead and getting caught off guard by a shortfall.

    This guide breaks down what a cash flow statement includes, how to prepare one, how to read it, and how it compares to an income statement and balance sheet with original examples throughout.

    What Is a Cash Flow Statement?

    A cash flow statement is a financial statement that reports the actual cash a business received and paid out during a set period. Unlike a budget or forecast, it reflects real, historical cash movement — not projections.

    It gives business owners, lenders, and investors a clear picture of:

    • How much cash the business generated from normal operations
    • How much cash was spent on or gained from long-term assets
    • How much cash came from or was paid toward loans, owner contributions, or dividends
    • Whether the business has enough cash on hand to cover its obligations

    Most companies prepare a cash flow statement monthly, quarterly, and annually, often alongside the income statement and balance sheet as part of a complete set of financial statements.

    One of the most important things a cash flow statement reveals is the difference between cash flow and accounting profit. Profit, shown on the income statement, includes revenue that hasn't been collected yet and expenses that haven't been paid yet. Cash flow only counts money that has actually changed hands.

    Simple example: A landscaping company invoices a client $8,000 for a completed project. That $8,000 counts as revenue immediately under standard accrual accounting, boosting reported profit. But if the client doesn't pay for 45 days, that $8,000 doesn't appear on the cash flow statement until the cash is actually received.

    Why Is a Cash Flow Statement Important?

    A cash flow statement matters because it answers a question profit alone can't: does the business have enough available cash to operate and grow?

    Here's what it helps with:

    • Liquidity monitoring – Shows whether there's enough cash to cover short-term obligations like payroll, rent, and supplier payments.
    • Financial planning – Helps owners anticipate slow periods and plan around them instead of reacting to them.
    • Business decisions – Supports decisions about hiring, expanding, or taking on new projects based on real cash availability, not just projected profit.
    • Expense management – Highlights where cash is being spent and whether spending patterns are sustainable.
    • Debt management – Shows how much cash is going toward loan payments and whether the business can comfortably keep up.
    • Growth planning – Helps determine whether the business can self-fund expansion or will need outside financing.

    A profitable business can still run into cash-flow trouble. Consider a Kansas-based equipment rental company that lands several large contracts in one quarter. Revenue and profit both look strong on the income statement. But if clients are on 60-day payment terms while the company still has to pay for fuel, maintenance, and wages every week, cash can run tight even as profit climbs. This is one of the most common and most misunderstood financial situations small businesses face.

    What Are the Three Sections of a Cash Flow Statement?

    Every cash flow statement is broken into three sections, each reflecting a different type of business activity.

    1. Cash Flow From Operating Activities

    This section shows cash generated or used by the core, day-to-day business — the activities that keep the business running.

    Cash inflows typically include:

    • Cash received from customers
    • Interest received

    Cash outflows typically include:

    • Payments to suppliers and vendors
    • Employee wages
    • Rent and utilities
    • Operating expenses like insurance or office supplies

    Operating cash flow is often considered the most important section because it reflects the cash-generating power of the business itself, separate from loans or asset sales.

    2. Cash Flow From Investing Activities

    This section reflects cash used for or generated by long-term assets and investments.

    Common examples include:

    • Purchasing equipment, vehicles, or property
    • Selling old equipment or property
    • Buying or selling investment securities
    • Acquiring another business

    A negative number here isn't automatically a bad sign — it often means the business is investing in growth, such as a contractor purchasing new machinery.

    3. Cash Flow From Financing Activities

    This section covers cash exchanged with lenders, owners, and shareholders.

    Common examples include:

    • Proceeds from a business loan
    • Loan or debt repayments
    • Owner or investor contributions
    • Issuing stock (for corporations)
    • Paying dividends to shareholders

    What It Shows
    Examples

    How Does a Cash Flow Statement Work?

    A cash flow statement connects a business's beginning and ending cash balance by tracking every cash inflow and outflow in between.

    The formula is straightforward:

    Beginning Cash + Net Change in Cash = Ending Cash

    Where "net change in cash" is the combined total of the operating, investing, and financing sections.

    In plain English: the statement starts with however much cash the business had on day one of the period, adds up everything that came in and went out across all three activity types, and arrives at exactly how much cash is left at the end of the period. That ending figure should match the actual cash balance shown on the balance sheet.

    How to Prepare a Cash Flow Statement Step by Step

    Step 1: Determine the beginning cash balance — Start with the cash balance from the end of the previous period — this becomes your opening figure.

    Step 2: Identify operating cash flows — List all cash received from customers and cash paid for day-to-day expenses like wages, rent, and supplies. Total these to get net operating cash flow.

    Step 3: Identify investing cash flows — List cash spent on or received from long-term assets, such as equipment purchases or asset sales. Total these to get net investing cash flow.

    Step 4: Identify financing cash flows — List cash received from loans or owner contributions, and cash paid out for loan repayments or dividends. Total these to get net financing cash flow.

    Step 5: Calculate the net change in cash — Add the three totals together: operating + investing + financing.

    Step 6: Calculate the ending cash balance — Add the net change in cash to the beginning cash balance. This final number should match the cash reported on the balance sheet for that same date.

    Businesses that keep accurate, up-to-date books throughout the year find this process far easier, since the underlying transaction data is already organized. This is one reason many growing companies move toward real-time bookkeeping instead of reconstructing records at tax time.

    Direct vs. Indirect Cash Flow Method

    There are two accepted ways to prepare the operating activities section of a cash flow statement: the direct method and the indirect method. Both produce the same final operating cash flow figure — they just get there differently.

    The core difference is simple: the direct method shows cash moving in and out in plain terms, while the indirect method reconciles net income to cash flow by adjusting for non-cash items like depreciation and timing differences in receivables and payables. Most small and mid-sized businesses use the indirect method because it's faster to prepare from standard accounting software.

    $180,000

    Ending Cash Balance

    Step-by-step explanation:

    Prairie Peak Construction started the quarter with $45,000 in cash. Operating activities brought in a net $30,000 ($180,000 collected from clients, minus $130,000 paid to suppliers and workers, minus $20,000 in operating expenses). Investing activities used a net $12,000, mainly for a new equipment purchase offset slightly by selling older machinery. Financing activities added a net $7,000 from a small loan minus repayments, plus an owner contribution.

    Adding the three sections together ($30,000 − $12,000 + $7,000) gives a net change in cash of $25,000. Adding that to the $45,000 beginning balance results in an ending cash balance of $70,000 — a healthy, positive quarter driven primarily by strong operating cash flow.

    How to Read and Analyze a Cash Flow Statement

    Reading a cash flow statement well means looking beyond the bottom-line number and understanding where cash is coming from.

    • Positive cash flow means more cash came in than went out during the period.
    • Negative cash flow means more cash went out than came in — which isn't always bad if it's driven by planned investment.
    • Operating cash flow should generally be positive and consistent; this is the clearest sign of a healthy core business.
    • Investing cash flow is often negative for growing businesses reinvesting in equipment or property.
    • Financing cash flow fluctuates depending on loans taken out or repaid, and owner activity.
    • Free cash flow (operating cash flow minus capital expenditures) shows how much cash is left after maintaining or growing the asset base — a useful measure of financial flexibility.

    When analyzing trends over multiple periods, business owners should watch for consistently negative operating cash flow, heavy reliance on financing activities to stay afloat, or a widening gap between reported profit and actual cash generated. A single unusual quarter isn't necessarily a concern, but a repeating pattern usually signals something worth investigating.

    Cash Flow Statement vs. Profit

    Cash flow and profit are related but fundamentally different measures.

    Net income (profit) is calculated using accrual accounting, which counts revenue when it's earned and expenses when they're incurred — regardless of when cash actually changes hands. Cash flow only reflects money that has physically been received or paid out.

    Example: A consulting firm completes $50,000 worth of work in March and records it as revenue that month, resulting in strong reported profit. But if the client doesn't pay until May, the firm's March cash flow statement won't reflect that $50,000 at all — even though the income statement does. If the firm has payroll and rent due in April, that gap between recognized profit and available cash can create real financial pressure.

    Can a Business Be Profitable but Have Negative Cash Flow?

    Yes — a business can be profitable on its income statement and still have negative cash flow. This happens when profit is tied up in things that haven't converted to cash yet, such as unpaid invoices, inventory, or recent large purchases.

    Example: A retail business in Kansas has a strong sales quarter, reporting solid profit. But during that same period, it invests heavily in restocking inventory ahead of a busy season, extends payment terms to a few large wholesale customers, and makes a down payment on a delivery van. Even though the income statement shows a profit, operating cash is tied up in unsold inventory and unpaid customer invoices, while investing activities used additional cash for the vehicle. The result: solid profit, tight cash.

    This is exactly why relying on profit alone to judge financial health can be misleading, and why tracking cash flow separately matters.

    Common Cash Flow Problems and Warning Signs

    Several patterns tend to signal cash flow trouble before it becomes a crisis:

    • Increasing accounts receivable – Customers are taking longer to pay, tying up cash.
    • Slow customer payments – Extended payment terms or chronic late payers drain available cash.
    • Excess inventory – Cash is sitting on shelves instead of in the bank.
    • High operating expenses – Costs are outpacing what operations bring in.
    • Large debt payments – Loan obligations are consuming a significant share of incoming cash.
    • Heavy capital spending – Frequent large equipment or asset purchases without matching cash inflows.
    • Negative operating cash flow – The core business isn't generating enough cash on its own, even if profitable on paper.
    • Over-reliance on financing – The business consistently needs loans or owner contributions just to stay current.
    • Unexpected cash shortages – Sudden gaps that weren't visible until bills came due.

    Any one of these on its own may be temporary. Several appearing together, or repeating over multiple periods, usually points to a structural cash flow issue that needs attention.

    How Can Businesses Improve Cash Flow?

    Practical steps that help both established and growing businesses strengthen cash flow include:

    • Invoice faster – Send invoices immediately after work is completed rather than batching them.
    • Improve collections – Follow up promptly on overdue invoices and consider incentives for early payment.
    • Manage inventory carefully – Avoid overstocking; order based on actual demand patterns.
    • Control unnecessary expenses – Regularly review recurring costs and subscriptions.
    • Forecast cash flow – Project upcoming cash needs so shortfalls can be planned for, not discovered.
    • Review payment terms – Negotiate longer terms with suppliers and shorter terms with customers where possible.
    • Monitor accounts receivable – Track aging invoices closely rather than only at month-end.
    • Plan major purchases – Time large equipment or asset purchases around periods of strong cash reserves.
    • Monitor cash flow regularly – Review it monthly at minimum, not just at tax season.

    For Kansas businesses balancing seasonal demand, weather-dependent work, or multiple concurrent projects, consistent cash flow monitoring tends to matter even more than for businesses with steady, predictable revenue.

    Cash Flow Management for Kansas Businesses

    Kansas businesses — from small service providers to growing regional companies — benefit from the same cash flow discipline that applies everywhere, with a few practical priorities worth emphasizing.

    Regular cash flow monitoring helps business owners catch tightening cash positions early, rather than after a payment is missed. Accurate bookkeeping ensures the numbers behind a cash flow statement are reliable in the first place; errors or delays in recording transactions can quietly distort the whole picture. Cash flow forecasting allows owners to plan around seasonal fluctuations, upcoming equipment purchases, or loan payments well in advance.

    Better visibility into financial performance and timely financial reporting also matter, especially for businesses juggling multiple projects, clients, or revenue streams at once. Understanding operating cash flow specifically — the cash generated by day-to-day work — gives Kansas business owners a clearer sense of whether the core business is genuinely self-sustaining, separate from loans or one-time cash injections.
